# Marketing Claims Guide

**A practical "safe to say / do NOT say" reference for selling the TI-84 Plus CE Python toolkit.**

Every claim here is backed by a primary source quoted in [`COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md`](COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md).
If a phrasing isn't on the "safe" list and you can't point to the quote that supports it, don't ship it.

Sources accessed 2026-08-12. Re-verify before each exam season — see the schedule at the end of the
research document.

---

## 1. The one-paragraph version

You have been underselling the AP story and you are right to have avoided the FE/PE story.

College Board states in writing, in two separate official documents, that **AP Exam calculator
memories do not need to be cleared**, and it lists the **TI-84 Plus CE Python by name** on its
approved calculator list. That is a genuinely strong, quotable, verifiable claim and you should use
it. What you must never do is compress it into "AP-approved programs" — College Board approves
*calculators*, not third-party software, and it runs no program-approval process at all. Meanwhile
the SAT, PSAT, ACT, IB, and NCEES rules all cut against in-exam program use, and the FE/PE ban is
absolute. So: **lead hard on AP and on coursework, stay silent or explicitly negative everywhere
else.**

## 2. The strongest *accurate* value proposition

Use this as the top-of-listing pitch. Every sentence is defensible.

> **52 ready-to-run Python programs for the TI-84 Plus CE Python Edition — built to save you time on
> homework, labs, and practice problems, and to make the calculator you already own genuinely
> faster.**
>
> The TI-84 Plus CE Python is on College Board's official list of calculators approved for AP®
> Exams, and College Board's published AP calculator policy states: *"You don't need to clear your
> calculators' memories before or after the exam."* College Board also states that *"Calculators
> with built-in physical constants, metric conversions, and physics, chemistry, or mathematics
> formulas are permitted."*
>
> Rules differ sharply by exam, though — the SAT, ACT, IB, and the NCEES FE/PE engineering exams all
> impose restrictions that these programs do not meet, and individual instructors set their own
> classroom rules. **Check your specific exam's policy before test day.** These are study and
> practice tools first.

Why this is strong: it is two direct quotes from the exam authority, plus a verifiable list
placement, plus an honest limitation. A buyer who reads it and then reads College Board's page finds
you were accurate — which is worth more in reviews than an inflated claim.

## 3. Safe to say / do NOT say

### 3.1 AP Exams

| ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|
| "The TI-84 Plus CE Python is on College Board's list of approved handheld graphing calculators for AP® Exams." | "AP-approved programs" / "AP-Exam-Legal" / "College Board–approved software" |
| Direct quote: *"You don't need to clear your calculators' memories before or after the exam."* (College Board, AP calculator policy) | "College Board explicitly allows calculator programs" — it does not say that anywhere |
| Direct quote: *"Calculator memories do not need to be cleared before or after the exam."* | "Certified for the AP® exam" / "AP® compliant" |
| Direct quote: *"Calculators with built-in physical constants, metric conversions, and physics, chemistry, or mathematics formulas are permitted."* | "Guaranteed to pass a proctor check" |
| "College Board does not require you to clear your calculator's memory for AP Exams — but always confirm with your AP coordinator and be ready for a proctor to inspect your calculator." | "Your proctor can't make you clear your memory" (adversarial framing; also invites an argument the student will lose) |
| "Built for AP® Calculus AB/BC, AP® Precalculus, AP® Statistics, AP® Physics 1/2/C, and AP® Chemistry coursework — all exams where a graphing calculator is allowed or required." | "Use these to answer AP free-response questions faster" (invites the cheating reading) |
| "Note: AP Calculus and AP Precalculus both have sections where no calculator is allowed at all." | Implying calculator tools help across a whole AP Calculus exam |

**The distinction that matters, stated plainly:** College Board's permissiveness is about *memory
contents in general*. It has approved your *hardware*, not your *software*. Say the first thing;
never imply the second.

**Watch item:** College Board has announced that for 2027, AP Biology and AP Environmental Science
will not allow "Handheld calculators with storage capabilities such as graphing calculators." AP
Macroeconomics and Microeconomics already carry similar language. Do not market to those subjects,
and watch whether the exclusion spreads.

### 3.2 SAT, PSAT/NMSQT, PSAT 10, PSAT 8/9

| ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|
| "The TI-84 Plus CE Python is not on College Board's prohibited-calculator list for the SAT Suite (only non-CAS calculators are allowed, and this model has no CAS)." | "SAT-approved" — **there is no approved list any more**, only a prohibited list |
| "SAT and PSAT rules require you to remove programs with algebra functionality and clear saved formulas before testing. Use these programs to prepare, not on test day." | "Take these into the SAT" / "SAT calculator programs" / anything implying in-test use |
| "Practising with your own TI-84 builds the calculator fluency that transfers to test day." | "Beat the digital SAT with stored formulas" |
| "The digital SAT includes a built-in Desmos graphing and scientific calculator in Bluebook, available for the whole Math section — you can also bring your own approved handheld." | Any claim that your programs work inside Bluebook (they don't — Bluebook's calculator is Desmos) |

**Why this is a hard no.** College Board's SAT rules say: *"You must remove programs that have
algebra functionality (e.g., solving equations, expanding algebraic expressions, factoring algebraic
expressions, etc.) from your calculator"* and *"You must remove any stored documents from your
calculator."* The SAT and PSAT *Testing Rules* pages add: *"Before testing, you will be asked to
clear all saved formulas on a calculator you bring."* Your equation solvers and your formula
flashcards are exactly what those sentences describe.

### 3.3 ACT and ACT WorkKeys

| ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|
| "The TI-84 family is not on ACT's prohibited-calculator list — but ACT restricts what programs may be stored, and these programs do not meet those restrictions. Remove them before an ACT." | "ACT-legal programs" / "ACT-approved" |
| "ACT requires all documents to be removed and limits permitted math programs to single-purpose programs of 25 logical lines or fewer. Use these for practice, then clear your calculator." | "Just put it in Press-to-Test" — ACT explicitly rejects this |
| Nothing about the ACT in your product titles or keywords at all is the safest option. | Any use of "ACT®" in a title, domain, or tag |

**Why.** ACT's CAS FAQ states permitted math programs must be *"single-purpose,"* *"must not be
lengthy. The limit is 25 logical lines of code,"* and *"Programs cannot call another program."*
Essentially every program in this library exceeds 25 lines, and several are multi-function menu
tools. ACT also says outright: *"Disabling programs – for example, putting the calculator in Press to
Test mode – is not enough."* Separately, ACT's Terms of Use state *"No use of any ACT trademark is
permitted without express written consent from ACT"* — so there is trademark risk on top of the
accuracy risk. Say nothing.

### 3.4 NCEES FE / PE / FS / PS — the absolute prohibition

| ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|
| "For engineering coursework — statics, circuits, thermodynamics, dynamics — where your instructor permits a graphing calculator." | **Anything** connecting this product to the FE or PE exam |
| "Heads up: the NCEES FE and PE exams do **not** allow the TI-84. NCEES permits only Casio fx-115/fx-991, HP 33s/35s, and TI-30X/TI-36X models. If you're studying for the FE, buy a TI-36X Pro and practise on that." | "FE exam prep" / "PE exam calculator" / "engineering licensure" |
| Nothing else. | Keywords or tags containing "FE exam," "PE exam," "NCEES," or "engineering licensure" |

**Why this is the single most dangerous claim you could make.** NCEES's rule is a model-name string
test: *"Any Texas Instruments calculator must have 'TI-30X' or 'TI-36X' in its model name."* "TI-84
Plus CE Python" contains neither. There is no exam mode, no discipline, and no exception that
changes this. A buyer who relied on an FE claim would be turned away at a Pearson VUE check-in
counter having paid an FE registration fee — a concrete, provable, quantifiable loss caused directly
by your advertising. That is the fact pattern that turns a marketing mistake into a refund demand or
a small-claims filing.

**The counterintuitive upside:** proactively telling engineering students the TI-84 is banned on the
FE, and naming the calculator they actually need, is excellent marketing. It costs you nothing (they
can't use the product there anyway), it is verifiably true, and it makes every other claim on your
page more credible.

### 3.5 IB, CLEP, GED, state tests

| Exam | ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|---|
| **IB** | "The TI-84 Plus CE Python is a permitted non-CAS device for IB Diploma exams, but IB requires third-party programs and stored notes to be removed or blocked, and schools must clear calculator memories. Study tool only." | "IB-approved programs" / any in-exam claim |
| **CLEP** | "CLEP Calculus and Precalculus put an on-screen **TI-84 Plus CE** in front of you — so the TI-84 workflow you build studying with these programs is the same workflow you'll use on the exam." | Any suggestion programs can be loaded — CLEP admits **no** personal calculator at all |
| **GED** | Nothing. | Anything. GED uses an on-screen TI-30XS; no graphing calculator pathway exists |
| **State tests** | Nothing generic. Policies vary by state; some (e.g. Alberta) explicitly approve the TI-84 Plus CE Python and even permit Press-to-Test to preserve stored programs. | Any blanket claim about "state standardized tests" |

The CLEP familiarity angle is a genuinely nice, under-used, and completely honest hook. Use it.

### 3.6 University and classroom exams — your real market

| ✅ Safe to say | ❌ Do NOT say |
|---|---|
| "Built for homework, labs, problem sets, and practice exams." | "Works on any college exam" |
| "Many instructors allow a graphing calculator with programs; many require a memory reset or Press-to-Test. Ask your instructor — and back up your programs first, so you can restore them after." | "Your professor will never know" / anything about concealment |
| "Ships with backup-and-restore instructions so you can strip your calculator for a proctored exam and put everything back in two minutes." | Implying the programs survive Press-to-Test (they very likely do **not** — see below) |

## 4. The Press-to-Test warning you must ship

This is a product-support fact, not a policy fact, and leaving it out will generate refunds.

TI's own documentation states that entering Press-to-Test deletes *"All variables stored in RAM and
in archived memory,"* and TI's Press-to-Test Guidebook says *"Other variables stored in RAM and in
archived memory (including AppVars) are deleted."* TI separately documents that Python programs on
the CE Python are stored as **Python AppVars**. Chaining those two facts: **Press-to-Test almost
certainly deletes Python programs outright, rather than temporarily hiding them the way it hides
Apps and TI-BASIC programs.** They will not come back when exam mode is exited.

TI does not spell this out in a single sentence on a CE Python page, so treat the mechanism as a
strongly-supported inference — but the buyer instruction is correct either way, so give it
unconditionally. Put this in every bundle README:

> **⚠️ Before you enter Press-to-Test, back up your programs.**
> Press-to-Test deletes variables in RAM and archived memory, including AppVars — and Python
> programs on the TI-84 Plus CE Python are stored as Python AppVars. Unlike Apps and TI-BASIC
> programs, they are **not** restored when you exit exam mode. Copy your `.py` files to your
> computer with TI Connect™ CE first, then re-send them after your exam. (A full memory reset
> deletes them too.) Your download stays in your account, so you can always re-install — but
> backing up first saves you the trip.

Framed well, this is a *feature*: "designed to be stripped and restored around a proctored exam in
two minutes" is a selling point, and it signals that you take integrity seriously.

### 6.2 Trademark rules to follow in product titles and copy

These come from College Board's published guidelines, which are the strictest of the set — following
them keeps you clear of everyone.

**Do:**

- Use marks as **adjectives modifying a noun**: "AP® Calculus course," "the SAT® exam." Never plural,
  never possessive.
- **Put your own brand first and make it more prominent.** College Board requires that its mark be
  "smaller in font, size, and color compared to your product/service name."
- Use the ® symbol on first prominent use of each mark on a page.
- Put the disclaimer **on the same surface** as the mark. College Board specifically says burying it
  in Terms & Conditions "does NOT satisfy these Guidelines."

**Don't:**

- **Don't lead a title with a mark.** College Board's own examples: *Incorrect* — "AP Calculus
  Guide"; *Correct* — "John Doe's Guide to AP® Calculus."
  → Rewrite `TI-84 Plus CE Python Calculus Toolkit` (fine — no CB mark) but never ship anything like
  `AP Calculus Program Pack`.
- **Don't put marks in keywords, tags, or meta tags.** College Board bars use of its marks "as part
  of company names, company taglines or slogans, social media pages, Internet domain names, web site
  addresses, or meta tags."
  → **Action item:** `bundles/LISTING_COPY.md` currently includes the tag `ap calculus study tool`.
  Remove or rewrite marketplace tags containing "ap," "sat," or "act" as exam-brand references.
- **Don't buy search ads on these marks.** College Board: "The College Board DOES NOT ALLOW third
  parties to leverage its brand, reputation and trademarks in search engine marketing and
  advertisements, such as Google AdWords, Facebook, etc."
- **Don't use any logo** — not the College Board acorn, not the ACT logo, not the TI logo, not a
  photo of a TI-84 lifted from TI's site. Use your own screenshots and your own device photos.
- **Don't register a domain or shop name** containing AP, SAT, ACT, TI, or TI-84.
- **Don't use "ACT" at all** in titles or tags. ACT's Terms of Use state flatly that "No use of any
  ACT trademark is permitted without express written consent," and the programs aren't ACT-usable
  anyway.

**A note on fair use, honestly stated.** College Board's guidelines assert that "Using a College
Board trademark in association with a consumer product or service without express permission is not
considered fair use." That is College Board's *position*, and it is broader than what US nominative
fair use doctrine probably requires — a large legitimate test-prep publishing industry operates on
the other reading. But marketplace takedowns are decided by marketplaces, not courts, and complying
costs you almost nothing. **Comply with the guidelines even where the law might allow more.**

## 7. Should the current disclaimer be loosened or tightened?

**Both — it is wrong in each direction, in different places.**

The current README disclaimer says:

> "Many standardized and proctored exams — AP Exams, the FE/PE exams, and many university
> midterms/finals — explicitly prohibit calculators that have been loaded with stored notes,
> formulas, or 'quiz' programs."

### Loosen: the AP claim in the current disclaimer is factually wrong

Naming **AP Exams** as an exam that "explicitly prohibits calculators loaded with stored notes,
formulas, or quiz programs" is **not accurate**. College Board says the opposite about memory
("Calculator memories do not need to be cleared before or after the exam") and affirmatively permits
calculators with built-in formulas. Being *over*-cautious is not automatically safe — this sentence
is an inaccurate statement about College Board's policy, it undersells your best market, and a
reasonably informed AP student who checks will conclude you don't know the policy. Fix it.

### Tighten: three real risks the current disclaimer misses

1. **NCEES is stated too softly.** "FE/PE exams … prohibit calculators loaded with stored notes"
   implies a *clean* TI-84 would be acceptable. It would not. The TI-84 is banned outright,
   regardless of what's on it. Say that explicitly.
2. **The SAT/PSAT and ACT are not mentioned at all** — and they are the two exams where your buyers
   are most likely to get into trouble, because the device is fine and the programs are not, which is
   exactly the situation a student is most likely to get wrong. Name them, with the specific rules.
3. **No Press-to-Test data-loss warning.** This is the most likely cause of an angry support email or
   a refund: a student enters exam mode as instructed, and their purchase is gone. Warn them.

### Also worth adding

- **IB** deserves a line — it is the most explicit "no stored programs" rule of any authority here.
- **A "check before every exam" instruction**, not just a one-time one. Policies change annually.
- **The trademark footer**, which is currently absent everywhere.

### Net effect

You end up **more accurate in both directions**: a genuinely strong, quotable AP claim you weren't
making, and specific, named warnings for SAT/PSAT, ACT, IB, and NCEES that you weren't making either.
Accuracy is the product here — it is also, conveniently, the better marketing.

## 8. Pre-publish checklist

Run this against any listing, page, ad, or bundle README before it goes live.

- [ ] No claim that any program is "approved," "certified," "legal," or "compliant" for any exam.
- [ ] Every quoted policy line is verbatim from the authority's own page, and the URL is cited or linked.
- [ ] AP claims are about the *calculator's* approval and the *memory* rule — never about program approval.
- [ ] The word "SAT," "PSAT," or "ACT" never appears in a context implying in-test program use.
- [ ] "FE," "PE," "NCEES," and "engineering licensure" do not appear except in an explicit
      *not permitted* warning.
- [ ] No exam-brand terms in tags, keywords, meta tags, shop name, or domain.
- [ ] No third-party logos or vendor product photography.
- [ ] Marks used as adjectives, with ®, and less prominent than your own product name.
- [ ] Non-affiliation footer present on the same surface as the marks.
- [ ] Press-to-Test backup warning present in the bundle README.
- [ ] Storefront disclaimer visible without the buyer expanding a section.
- [ ] Nothing in the copy sells concealment, evasion, or "beating" an exam.
- [ ] Policy sources re-verified within the last 6 months (see the re-verification schedule in
      [`COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md`](COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md) §10).

## 9. Three "do NOT say" traps, ranked by how much damage they do

1. **"FE exam" / "PE exam" / "NCEES" in any affirmative context.** The TI-84 is flatly banned. A buyer
   relying on this is turned away at a Pearson VUE counter having paid an FE registration fee — a
   concrete, provable, quantifiable loss caused directly by your advertising. Worst realistic
   downside of anything on this page.
2. **"AP-Exam-Legal" / "AP-approved programs."** The underlying policy really is permissive, which
   makes this trap seductive — but College Board approves *calculators*, not third-party software,
   and operates no program-approval process. The claim is false as written, it uses a College Board
   mark in exactly the way its guidelines forbid, and it is trivially checkable by anyone who wants
   to complain about you. You do not need it: the true version is nearly as strong.
3. **"SAT calculator programs" / anything implying in-test use on the SAT, PSAT, or ACT.** These are
   your highest-volume search terms and therefore the most tempting. They are also the ones where
   the exam authority's rules are most explicit against you (*"remove programs that have algebra
   functionality"*; *"clear all saved formulas"*; ACT's 25-line limit), and where a student who
   believes you gets flagged in a high-stakes college-admissions test. Don't.
